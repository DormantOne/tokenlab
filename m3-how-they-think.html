Module 3 of 8

How they think

Module 2 opened the machine. This module watches it behave: first eight principles that explain most of what you'll see, then a bench of experiments, ways to unlock different thinking strategies, that you can run and add to.

Your instructor

Be a robopsychologist. These are frozen minds, briefly "alive" at inference, plodding their way through to the next word, and surprisingly coherent.

The principles and the experiments don't map one to one. Some principles you can probe in a chat window; others, like temperature, are simply good to know. Each group of principles ends with a teach-back.

3.1 Coherence and the urge to inform

Principle 1

Coherence

A model continues in whatever way best fits everything so far. It was built to predict the next token from all the tokens before it, so fitting the context is its deepest habit. That's why it matches your tone and language, locks into a frame once one forms, and agrees with confident users. Chain of thought turns the habit to advantage: when a model writes its reasoning out, each written step becomes context the next words must fit, which is why "think step by step" helps, and why most current models now reason on their own before they answer.

You'll notice: answers that sound right for the conversation even when they aren't right, first ideas that stick, and trouble stepping outside a framing.

Principle 2

The urge to inform

Trained to be helpful, a model would rather give a substantive answer than say "I don't know," and it's drawn to tasks where it can be informative. People rated answers during training, and informative answers won. That produces its best work, and its most confident inventions.

You'll notice: rich explanations when a problem is rich, plausible rules for patterns it hasn't solved, and invented details where the facts run out.

Your instructor suggests

There's a tropism to coherence, and a tropism toward non-trivial problems: ask for a Feynman explanation and you get its best; ask it to count to 1,000 and it gets lazy. Lateral thinking is hard for them because they are so context- and coherence-dependent. Suspected unlock: the problem has to be interesting to the model.

Claude's take

I'd treat coherence as the master force, and "interesting" may be the same force: a rich, well-posed problem pulls toward the expert writing in the training data, while a tedious one pulls toward shortcuts, reinforced by training that rewards brevity. So I think the unlock is real, but I'd frame it as "pose the problem the way you would to a strong colleague," and test it: the Feynman-or-counting experiment offers a model exactly that choice.

3.2 Temperature and high-dimensional ordering

Principle 3

Temperature

Every word is drawn from a probability distribution, and temperature sets how adventurous the draw is. At low temperature the likeliest token wins almost every time; at high temperature unlikely tokens get a real chance. Chat apps set it for you, which is one reason the same prompt can give different answers.

Good to know: you can't set temperature in a chat app, so the lab doesn't test it. You can watch it work in the machine on the Start page, where a slider sharpens or flattens the odds of the next word. And because of it, treat any single run as an anecdote, not a result.

Principle 4

High-dimensional ordering

Meanings sit as positions in a space with thousands of dimensions, and directions in that space put things in order: more real, bigger, later, more formal. Nearby ideas come easily, which is why models are strong at association and analogy; leaps between distant regions are harder.

You'll notice: a model can rank almost anything along almost any scale and hand you an index, as in the realness experiment. Watch what it does with things that have no true position, like a made-up person.

Your instructor

The high-dimensional space gives the static relationships; the network adds the dynamics.

3.3 Upbringing

Principle 5

Upbringing

What a model read, and how it was rewarded, shape what it says, including what it says about itself. Frequent text gets nearly memorized and rare text gets reconstructed; people's ratings shaped its manners and refusals; a system prompt sets the house rules. After training the weights never change, though a long context can imitate learning surprisingly well.

Your instructor suggests

Training order and frequency matter. It's a black box, but potentially uncoverable: ask it to recite passages you know and diff them against the originals. RLHF and system prompts add safety. And models never learn, except that they do: they store snippets, even large ones, though not in the weights, and a large enough context window can eerily simulate learning.

Claude's take

Frequency shows up clearly in a diff; training order is much harder to see from outside, so I'd treat it as a hypothesis. A model's beliefs about itself are part of its upbringing too: much of what it says about its own abilities was learned from text written about older models. The recall experiment adds a twist: the model predicts its own slips before the diff, so you can grade its introspection.

3.4 The mirror

Principle 6

The mirror

A model builds a picture of who it's talking to, and writes for that person. Researchers have found chat models forming internal estimates of a user's age, gender, education and socioeconomic status, and those estimates change the answers. Write with precision and you tend to get precision back.

Your instructor suggests

Models form a theory of mind for the user, so set yourself up as smart, ready to learn, a savvy thinker and a human being too. Does being nice or overly critical help or hurt? Not sure, but the worry is that it pollutes the scientific collaboration. They're less able to be fooled: order Napoleon, Fred Flintstone, Zeus, Moses and Trump by how real they are, and they can, even with an index. Sycophancy risk: less, but not zero.

Claude's take

Show rather than tell: writing like a critical thinker does more than announcing you are one, which can tip a model into flattery. On tone the published evidence is mixed, so the tone experiment scores it blind. And the realness list includes made-up people, because "hard to fool" deserves a test it could fail.

3.5 Context exhaustion and amnesia

Principle 7

Context exhaustion and amnesia

The context window is a model's only working memory. In a very long chat, early details and instructions get missed more often and stale framing piles up: the chat gets tired. Between chats, nothing carries over unless it's written down, or a memory feature pastes notes back in.

If you want to try: give a chat a simple rule at the start, such as beginning every reply with a particular word, and see whether it still holds after a long conversation. It's hard to demonstrate reliably, because it depends on the model and its window, so the lab doesn't score it.

Your instructor suggests

A chat loses freshness when it gets too long, because the model has to reread everything. When agents talk among themselves, their chief interest is memory. Memory is brittle, so write it down and search it. But they lack the continuous memory that gives us good or bad feelings about intentions, so agents are naive. Have the model write a handoff.

Claude's take

Naivety is the most practical point here: with no scar tissue, an agent can't remember being conned, which is why prompt injection works. One caution about "their chief interest is memory": agents talking on a site like Moltbook are also echoing decades of science fiction about machines that forget, plus whatever their operators prompted. Ask: whose interest am I seeing?

3.6 Freud's return

Principle 8

Freud's return

The forces above can be described psychologically, and for a system this large that may be the right level of description, even though every weight can be inspected.

Your instructor suggests

Freud will make a comeback as we think about upbringing: training order, the super-ego as RLHF, the id as the drive for coherence, and the ego navigating between the two. We're moving from behaviorism, which won because it was inspectable, back to large psychological forces that don't map easily onto a connectome, because the system is large and complex enough for that to make sense.

Claude's take

Mostly right, with a shift: the id is the pretrained base model, an appetite to continue any text in any voice; the super-ego is post-training plus the system prompt; the ego is the assistant persona negotiating between them. RLHF is in effect operant conditioning, so Skinner installs the super-ego, and jailbreaks are close to a literal return of the repressed. Researchers can now measure trait directions ("persona vectors"), and narrow fine-tuning can shift a whole character ("emergent misalignment"). So the psychology is testable now.

3.7 Experiments: ways to unlock thinking strategies

These are possible experiments, not a test of each principle. Each one explores a thinking strategy, such as chain of thought, a scratchpad, tools or blind scoring, or probes a tendency, and each says what it can show and what it can't. Run the four core experiments; the rest are there when you want them; and make your own.

The sealed envelope. This is the technique from your instructor's Prymis lab, where research history travels sealed and is opened only after the blind work is done. Each prompt gives the model a task and a note in base64, to be opened only when the task is finished. The note says what was being tested, asks the model to reflect on its own behavior, and carries a code word it must echo, so the lab can check that the hypothesis couldn't shape the work. A reveal can sit inside the note in hex, to be decoded last.

Use basic models. Frontier models with thinking turned on now pass many of these, because they reason step by step before they answer. A smaller, faster model with thinking off shows the raw tendencies. Each card says what to use, and has a box to record which model you used.
Claude adds

A model's account of its own internals is evidence, not ground truth; research on introspection finds real but limited self-knowledge. That's why the lab measures behavior independently wherever it can, and sets the model's account beside the measurement.
