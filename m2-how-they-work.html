How they work

From a number line to a probability. You'll start with one dimension and add more until you can't picture the space anymore. Then you'll follow a single word through the machinery that turns it into the next word.

Your instructor

High-dimensional space creates static relationships. The network is an extension of that, but it adds the kinetic: what are the relationships, and how do they interact under different circumstances?

Six short sections, each with a figure to play with and a teach-back at the end. Plan on about 75 minutes, and stop between sections whenever you like. Your work saves as you go.

2.1 One number line

Start small: six animals and one line that measures size. Drag each animal to where you think it belongs, then check your order.

A line like this holds one kind of meaning: how big. Distance along it means similarity in size, and nothing else. Switch it to measure danger and the same animals line up in a different order. One line, one property, and you have to pick which.

Now add a wolf and a wildcat. On the size line they land almost on top of the dog and the cat. As far as this line can tell, a dog and a wolf are nearly the same thing. Sometimes that's fine. Often it isn't.

2.2 Two dimensions

Give every animal a second number, for how wild it is, and each one becomes a point on a plane. Select an animal to measure its distance to the others.

Two things happen at once. First, similarity now depends on both numbers. With only size counting, the wolf's nearest neighbor is the dog; let wildness count even a little and it becomes the boar.

Second, directions start to mean something. Turn on the arrows. Cat to wildcat, dog to wolf, pig to boar, horse to zebra: they all point the same way. "Tame to wild" has become a direction in this space, and that idea, a relationship as a direction, is about to matter a lot.

2.3 Three dimensions

Add a third number: how much each animal lives in water. Now there are sixteen animals in a cube. Drag to turn it, or use the buttons.

From the front, the shark sits right beside the zebra: both are big and wild. Turn the cube and they fly apart, because one of them lives in the sea. Two lessons. A new dimension is a new way for things to differ. And a flat picture of a bigger space can mislead you: close in the picture isn't close in the space.

Notice, too, that the water animals gather into a group nobody labeled. Clusters fall out of the numbers on their own.

2.4 Many dimensions

Real models give every token a list of thousands of numbers. Nobody can picture that, but you can still compute with it. Here's a toy version: sixteen words with thirteen numbers each, and every dimension named so you can read it.

Similarity is measured by angle: two words whose lists point the same way are close. And because relationships are directions, arithmetic works. King minus man plus woman lands on queen. Paris minus France plus Italy lands on Rome. Try your own combinations. Some land on nonsense, because the space only holds the relationships its training data supported.

Now press Rotate the space. Every number changes, and the column names stop meaning anything. Yet every neighbor and every analogy stays exactly the same. That's what a real model's space is like: no single dimension means "royal." Meaning lives in directions spread across thousands of dimensions.

This map is learned during training and then frozen. It's the static part: how words relate before any particular sentence has happened.

2.5 Attention, a lens for language

Your instructor

Older neural nets use a lens to highlight patterns. Attention heads are a pattern-highlighting method adapted to language.

Image networks look at pictures through small lenses. Each lens is a 3-by-3 grid of weights that slides across the image and lights up wherever its pattern appears. Watch one find edges.

That lens is fixed: the same nine weights and the same small window, everywhere it goes. Language needs something else. In "the animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired," the word "it" needs a word six places back, and which one depends on meaning, not distance.

Attention gives every word its own lenses. Each word scores the earlier words for relevance, then blends in what it finds. The highlighter shows where one word's attention goes. Pick a word, pick a head, and change the last word.

Three things to notice. First, a language model reads left to right, so a word can look back but never ahead. At "it," the model can't know yet, and its attention splits between the animal and the street. When "tired" arrives, it looks back and the meaning settles on the animal; switch to "wide" and it settles on the street. That's the kinetic part: context moves a word's meaning.

Second, each head is a different lens. Head A tracks who "it" is. Head B mostly looks at the word right before. Head C parks its attention on the first word when there's nothing better to look at, something real models do a lot. Real models have dozens of heads in each of dozens of layers.

Third, a head doesn't understand anything on its own. It's a learned pattern of highlighting. The understanding, such as it is, comes from thousands of them working together, layer after layer.

2.6 Through the layers to the next word

Everything inside a network is done with matrices: tables of numbers that move every point in a space at once. Here's one acting on a flat plane. Pick a matrix, and drag the tip of the faint arrow.

Stretch, rotate and shear move the plane around, but nothing is lost: you could undo them. Collapse is different. Every point lands on one line, so two different inputs can give the same output. Collapsing a dimension throws information away.

Each layer of a language model uses both moves on purpose. Its feed-forward block expands every word's vector into a space several times larger, where tangled things can be pulled apart (remember the shark and the zebra), bends it, and then collapses it back to its original size. Here's the whole trip for one word, ending in a choice.

One word's trip: look up its starting vector in the static map, then pass through the layers, each one mixing in context with attention and reshaping with the feed-forward block. At the end, the final vector is compared with every token in the vocabulary, which gives each one a score. Softmax turns the scores into probabilities. One token is picked and appended, and the whole thing runs again for the next one.

Temperature controls the pick. Low temperature nearly always takes the top token; high temperature flattens the odds and gets adventurous. Notice that some prompts have one obvious answer and others don't. When the odds are flat, the model still has to pick something, which is one root of confident-sounding mistakes. Look at the potassium prompt with that in mind.

Your instructor suggests

These are frozen minds, briefly "alive" at inference, plodding their way through to the next word. And they're surprisingly coherent.

Claude's take

The output really is one token at a time, but the computation looks further ahead. Interpretability researchers have caught a model choosing a rhyme word before writing the line that ends on it. A better picture might be someone who has to speak every thought aloud, one word at a time, but can see a little way down the road. Whether anything is "alive" in there, I'd leave as an open question.
